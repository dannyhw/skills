Yes. The MIT license allows commercial use, including in closed-source products. You don't have to open-source your own code.

**What you have to do**

1. **Keep the copyright notice and the MIT license text.** The license requires that "the above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be included in all copies or substantial portions of the Software."
2. **Include them in what you ship.** For a distributed product (desktop app, mobile app, on-prem install, bundled JS), the notices must be somewhere users can reach them. Common places are an "Open Source Licenses" or "About" screen, a `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES` file, or the docs.
3. **For a hosted service or SaaS,** you aren't distributing the software. Strictly, the attribution requirement usually isn't triggered. Many companies include the notices anyway.

**What you don't have to do**

- Publish your source code.
- License your product under MIT.
- Notify or pay the author.
- Contribute changes back. You can modify the library freely.

**Caveats**

- **Check transitive dependencies.** The library may pull in dependencies under other licenses (GPL, AGPL, etc.). Each has its own obligations.
- **No warranty.** The software is provided "as is," so you have no recourse if it breaks or causes harm.
- **Bundlers can strip license comments.** Minifiers and bundlers sometimes remove the notices, so make sure they survive in the output or are collected separately.
- **Dual-licensed or modified "MIT" text.** Confirm the license file is the plain MIT text, not a variant with extra clauses.

**Practical tip:** Use a tool to generate the notices file automatically. Examples are `license-checker` or `license-report` for npm, `cargo-about` for Rust, and `pip-licenses` for Python. It's also worth running a license scan in CI so a copyleft dependency can't slip in unnoticed.

This is general information, not legal advice. If the product is commercially significant, have your legal team confirm your compliance process.
