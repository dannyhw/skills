Yes. The MIT licence allows use in closed-source, commercial products. You don't have to release your own source code.

**What you must do**

1. **Keep the copyright notice.** Include the library's copyright line.
2. **Keep the licence text.** Include the full MIT permission notice.

The licence says these must appear in "all copies or substantial portions of the Software". In practice, this means:

- **Shipped apps (mobile, desktop, firmware):** Add an "Open source licences" or "Third-party notices" screen or file. List each library with its copyright line and licence text.
- **Web apps:** Link to a notices page. Also keep the notices in any bundled JavaScript, or in a separate file that ships with it.
- **Server-side only:** Strictly, you may not be distributing the software, so the duty may not apply. Many teams still keep the notices in the repo or docs.

**What you don't have to do**

- Publish your source code.
- Licence your own code under MIT.
- Ask the author's permission.
- Pay anything.

**Things to be aware of**

- **No warranty.** The software comes "as is". The author isn't liable for problems.
- **Dependencies have their own licences.** The library may pull in other packages under different licences, such as GPL. Check the full dependency tree.
- **Don't remove notices from the source files** if you copy code into your own.
- **Check it really is MIT.** Read the `LICENSE` file, not just the `package.json` or README. Some projects are dual-licensed or have extra terms.

**Practical tips**

- Use a tool to generate the notices file. For JavaScript, try `license-checker` or `license-report`. For other languages, there are similar tools.
- Add a licence check to your CI, so a non-permissive dependency doesn't slip in.

I'm not a lawyer. If this is a high-stakes product, or you're going through due diligence for a sale, ask your legal team to confirm.
